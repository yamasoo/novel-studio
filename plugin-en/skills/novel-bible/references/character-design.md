# Character design method

Follow this when generating or completing characters. The point is "can carry scenes", not filling in a résumé.

## 1. Ask about function first, then the profile

For each character, first answer: **what does this person do in the story?**

Common function slots:
- **Lead**: whom the reader identifies with; the engine of the story.
- **Opponent**: stops the lead from getting what they want. Best with their own reasonable goal, not simply bad.
- **Mirror**: took the road the lead did not, letting the reader see what the lead's choice costs.
- **Mentor / resource provider**: gives the lead ability, information or opportunity, and usually has their own calculations.
- **Love interest**: pulls on the lead emotionally and drives the romance line.
- **Stepping stone**: surpassed or humbled by the lead, showing the lead's growth. Common in serial fiction but still needs a believable motive; must not merely be stupid.
- **Disruptor**: creates surprises, upsets plans, adds variables.

Do not put two characters in the same function slot or one becomes decoration. One character may hold two functions.

## 2. The four fields that make a character stand up

These four are the most important in the profile; the rest is detail:

1. **Wants**: a concrete goal that can be blocked. "Wants to get stronger" is too vague; "wants to place in the top ten at the guild trials and win back the slot taken from her" can become a scene.
2. **Fears**: what would make them choose wrongly.
3. **Flaw**: a weakness of character that costs them and worries the reader. A character with no flaw has no room to grow.
4. **Bottom line**: what they will never do. The bottom line is the anchor of consistency: when you write them breaking it, there must be enough pressure and cost.

## 3. The voice card (so everyone does not talk alike)

Each main character should be able to answer:
- **Sentence length**: talkative or terse? Full sentences or a few words?
- **Register**: refined, coarse, jargon, dialect, internet slang?
- **Habitual way of applying pressure**: reasoning, a cold smile, dredging up the past, playing pitiful, silence, going straight to blows?
- **With people of different standing**: how does speech differ toward superiors, equals and subordinates?
- **Catchphrase or signature pattern**: write it if there is one; do not force one. Keep catchphrases rare; too frequent and they become a cheap label.

Test: cover the speaker's name in the dialogue. Can the reader guess who it is?

## 4. Looks: keep only identifying marks

Do not write ID-photo descriptions (height, hair colour, eye colour, clothing, all listed). Keep one or two features a reader will remember and that can be used in the plot: an old scar, gloves always worn, a slight limp, clearing the throat before speaking.

## 5. Relationships need warmth and direction

The relationship field should say more than "master and pupil" or "enemy":
- **Warmth**: trust, dependence, wariness, using each other, hostility …
- **Direction**: is the relationship getting better or worse?
- **The unspoken**: what does each side hide?

## 6. Generating supporting characters in bulk

When the author says "generate N supporting characters":
1. First confirm the use: where in the plot do they sit and what function do they carry? Ask if unclear.
2. For each one write only "one-line function + wants + flaw + two or three voice-card lines + relation to the lead"; no long backstory.
3. Deliberately make them differ from each other: pull apart their status, way of speaking and stance.
4. Put them all in `設定/草稿/` for the author to pick.
5. Avoid names that sound or look alike, and names too like existing characters (check the glossary and the character folder). Avoid the stock names that language models over-produce.

## 7. Common faults

- The profile is full, yet not one entry can become conflict.
- All characters are clever, articulate and rational.
- The villain has no goal of their own and exists only to block the lead.
- The profile says "cold", yet the text shows them caring for people every time they appear. A profile must land in behavior and not live only in the sheet.
