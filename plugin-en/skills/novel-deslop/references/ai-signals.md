# AI tells in English fiction

Original list, written for this tool. The categories follow the general patterns that writers and editors commonly describe; the wording and examples are our own. A tell is a **pattern repeated without a reason**, not a banned word. One occurrence is usually fine; a pile of them in one chapter is the problem. Ask of each: if I cut it, is any information lost?

## 1. Negation flip ("not X but Y")
- "It wasn't fear. It was anger." / "Not because he wanted to, but because he had to."
- The setup knocks down a target nobody proposed. State the real thing, then give one concrete reason.

## 2. Stock micro-expressions
- A smile tugged at the corner of her mouth; something flickered in his eyes; her jaw tightened; knuckles whitened; a muscle jumped in his cheek; she let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.
- Several in one beat read like a template. Keep one action that moves the scene.

## 3. Body-as-emotion clichés
- A shiver ran down her spine; her heart hammered; his stomach dropped; the air left her lungs; ice pooled in her veins.
- Use only when the body reaction is surprising for this character, and then once.

## 4. Telling the reader what to feel
- "She felt a surge of grief." "A wave of relief washed over him." "He realized, then, how much he had lost."
- The narrator names the emotion the scene should have produced. Replace with something the character does, sees or avoids.

## 5. Explaining the subtext
- "…, which was her way of saying she forgave him." "The silence said everything."
- Let the reader work it out. If they can't, the scene needs a clearer beat, not a caption.

## 6. Tidy triplets and parallelism
- "She was tired, she was angry, and she was done." Three adjectives, three beats, three clauses, every time.
- Vary the count. Two, four, or one precise thing.

## 7. Aphorism and mic-drop lines
- "Some doors, once closed, never open again." "Grief is just love with nowhere to go."
- A quotable line that summarizes the paragraph. Cut, or make it the character's own speech with a reason.

## 8. Reflective or prophetic chapter endings
- "Whatever came next, she would face it." "Little did he know…" "It was only the beginning." "And for the first time in years, she felt hope."
- End on an image, an action or a line of dialogue.

## 9. Filler adverbs and hedges
- slowly, softly, quietly, gently, suddenly, simply, just, somehow, almost, seemed to, a little, a bit, a hint of, a flicker of, something like
- Hedging makes every beat vague. Keep one where the vagueness is the point.

## 10. Stock similes and metaphors
- like a physical blow; thick enough to cut with a knife; the weight of the world; a storm brewing; time seemed to slow; a symphony of…
- Use a comparison this point-of-view character would actually make from their own life.

## 11. Stock dialogue tags and speech patterns
- said softly / said quietly / murmured / whispered (used as the default), voice barely above a whisper, voice laced with…
- "Said" is almost invisible; use it. Put the manner in an action beat.
- Brochure dialogue: characters state their feelings and plans in full, grammatical, polite sentences; every question gets a direct answer.

## 12. Canned reaction shots
- She froze. He paused. Silence stretched between them. For a long moment, neither spoke. The room went still.
- Reaction with no content. Cut, or replace with the thing the character does because of what was said.

## 13. Significance inflation
- "Everything had changed." "It was more than a (noun); it was a (noun)." "This was the moment that would define her."
- Let the events carry the weight.

## 14. Official and abstract register
- furthermore, moreover, in essence, ultimately, a testament to, navigate (as in navigate grief), tapestry, delve, testament, myriad, palpable, unspoken
- Report-style words in a story. Use the plain word a person in this scene would think in.

## 15. Names and settings on autopilot
- Stock names (Elara, Kael, Lyra, Thorne, Sarah Chen, Marcus), stock town names, a lighthouse, a quiet coastal town, "the old oak tree".
- Pick names that belong to this book's world, and vary initials and syllable counts.

## 16. Rhythm tells (the script checks these)
- **Burst**: four or more very short sentences in a row to fake tension. Fragment. Fragment. Fragment.
- **Uniform length**: six sentences in a row of nearly equal length.
- **Em dashes**: more than about one per 200 words of narration.
- **Name cycling**: one character called by four or more different labels in a chapter.

## What not to do about all this
- Don't ban the words wholesale. A tell is a repeated, unmotivated use.
- Don't replace each flagged item with a synonym from the same family.
- Honor the author's style note: some genres (romance, pulp, fairy tale) lean on stock devices on purpose.
