# How to write a chapter card

The chapter card is the only plot blueprint for the chapter text. novel-write writes by it, so write the card to the point where, holding it, you cannot write in the wrong direction, but do not write it as chapter text.

## 1. The four questions (required for every chapter)

1. **What does the lead want to do in this chapter?**
2. **Who or what stops them?**
3. **What is the situation after this chapter?**
4. **Why will the reader keep reading?**

If question 3 cannot be answered, or the answer is "nothing changes", the chapter should be cut, merged or compressed.

## 2. Minimal structure of a scene

Every scene needs at least a **goal, an obstacle, a change**. A scene with no change (just information, just small talk) should be cut, merged into another scene, or given a small change.

## 3. How to write plot points

- List them in the order they happen, one per line.
- Write "what happens", not "how to write it":
  - Good: "Callum publicly breaks the engagement; Ines doesn't cry and asks whose seal is on the contract"
  - Bad: "A flash of cold passed through Ines's eyes, and the corner of her mouth curved in faint mockery …" (that is chapter text, not a card)
- Pin down the key choices and their results; leave process detail for the text to play with.
- If a line must appear verbatim (an oath, the contents of a letter, a system message), add "exact line: …" after the plot point.
- 3-6 plot points per chapter is most common. More than 8 usually means too much is crammed in; consider splitting into two chapters.

## 4. Function and target emotion

Give each chapter one main function:
- **Advance**: the main line takes a step
- **Payoff**: comeback, reversal, a gain
- **Tension**: push and pull in romance or relationships
- **Reveal**: new information, a new clue, a corner of the truth
- **Set-up**: lay conditions for a later burst
- **Bridge**: change of scene, rest, daily life (not too many in a row)

Then name the main feeling the reader should have afterwards (satisfaction, tension, curiosity, ache, sweetness, frustration). If three chapters in a row have the same feeling, consider adjusting.

## 5. Chapter-ending hooks

Plan where every chapter stops. Hook types:

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Open question | Throw out a question the reader must have answered | The envelope held one blank sheet, yet on the back was her mother's private seal |
| Crisis | A character is in danger | The footsteps outside stopped |
| Reversal | Break what the reader just came to believe | The man who rescued her wore the Brandt crest on his cuff |
| New goal | Give a clear direction for the next stage | In three days the guild trials open; the top ten get into the archive |
| Light teaser | No fanfare, only a light pointing to the next scene | "Let's go." He turned toward the end of the quay |
| Emotional afterglow | After a heavy emotional scene, a last beat to linger on | (suits the chapter after a climax) |

Rules:
- **Not every chapter a crisis**: using the same hook repeatedly numbs readers and looks contrived. Rotate, alternating strong and soft.
- **A hook is a promise**: the next chapter must catch it. A hook that is not caught uses up the reader's trust.
- **Do not tie off every thread**: leave at least one line unresolved at the end of the chapter.
- Hooks should be strong in high-pressure or advancing chapters; a bridge chapter needs only a stage goal or a small suspense.

## 6. Foreshadowing operations

In the card, state:
- which foreshadowing is **newly planted** in this chapter (give a provisional ID; register it formally after writing)
- which foreshadowing is **advanced or lightly touched**
- which foreshadowing is **paid off** (paying off important foreshadowing is "ask the author first"; confirm the author agrees before scheduling it in a card)

## 7. Prohibitions

Write down what **must not** happen in this chapter, especially:
- truths that cannot be revealed yet
- characters who cannot meet or recognise each other yet
- abilities or trump cards that cannot be used yet

This is a guard rail for novel-write; it stops the AI from spending later plot early in order to write something exciting.

## 8. Laying out cards in batches

One plot unit (5-15 chapters) at a time is best. When done:
1. Check the unit's emotional curve: pressure, release, a small climax at the end of the unit.
2. Check that hook types vary.
3. Check question 3 of each chapter's four questions to make sure the situation changes every chapter.
4. Check foreshadowing: is whatever should be lightly touched scheduled?
5. Show the author the unit overview (one row per chapter: title + one line + function), not every card in detail at once.
