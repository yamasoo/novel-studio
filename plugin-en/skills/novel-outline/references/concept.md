# Story-core diagnosis

Before writing an outline, confirm that the idea can carry the intended length. When an idea cannot be squeezed into the minimal skeleton, do not rush to lay out chapters.

## 1. Minimal skeleton (five questions)

1. **Who is the lead?** One sentence: identity + situation.
2. **What do they want?** A concrete goal that can be blocked and whose progress the reader can see.
3. **What blocks them?** An opponent, an institution, their own flaw, the environment.
4. **Why will the reader want to see them win (or struggle)?** The empathy point: wrong done to them, injustice, longing, contrast.
5. **How does the story keep running?** The story engine (see below).

If any of the five cannot be answered, talk it through with the author first.

## 2. Selling points: what readers are really consuming

Serial readers keep up for a recurring kind of emotional satisfaction. You should be able to say which one to three this book mainly offers:

| Type of selling point | What the reader gets | Typical technique |
|---|---|---|
| Comeback / reversal | exhilaration | looked down on → ability shown → the onlookers' reactions |
| Hidden strength | anticipation + exhilaration | the lead is underrated, the reader knows the truth, pressure builds and bursts |
| Levelling up | sense of achievement | clear ranks, a visible gain at each stage |
| Romantic tension | flutter, longing | closeness and obstacle alternate, misunderstanding and reconciliation |
| Mystery / suspense | curiosity, intellectual satisfaction | clues laid fairly up front, a "so that's it" reveal |
| Building / homesteading | reassurance, accumulation | from nothing to something, growth in numbers and scale |
| Identity contrast | surprise | a hidden identity revealed layer by layer, others' perceptions flipping |

The selling point sets the rhythm: payoff stories need dense release, mysteries need evenly spaced clues, romances need push and pull.

## 3. The story engine

A story engine is a mechanism that "naturally produces new conflict every so often". A long book without an engine starts to run out of things to do by chapter twenty or thirty.

A good engine usually has these traits:
- **Repeatable**: the same mechanism fits different scenes and different opponents.
- **Escalating**: each repetition raises the stakes or the stage.
- **Tied to the main line**: each round's result moves the main line one step.

Examples:
- "Each time she unlocks another layer of the token, stronger pursuers appear and a piece of her past comes out": repeatable, escalating, tied to the main line.
- "The lead is strong and beats bad guys everywhere": repeatable, but it neither escalates nor ties to the main line, and soon tires.

## 4. The reader contract

The reader contract is the promise made to readers in the opening, which the rest must keep:
- **Genre promise**: once labelled a sweet romance, it cannot turn grim to the end.
- **Lead promise**: if the opening shows the lead as smart, they cannot become stupid because the plot needs it.
- **Pace promise**: if the opening is dense with payoffs, there cannot suddenly be ten chapters with no release.

List the contract in `設定/故事核心.md`. When writing the outline and cards, check against the contract for breaches.

## 5. Judging length

| Idea features | Suits |
|---|---|
| One core reversal, a change in one relationship | short story (under 10k words) |
| A full growth or revenge arc, little need to widen the stage | novella (30k-80k) |
| Levelling steps, a stage that widens layer by layer, several side lines to alternate | long novel (100k+) |

When the author wants a long book but the idea only supports a novella, say so honestly and propose ways to extend it (add levelling steps, add side lines, widen the stage) for the author to decide.

## 6. Diagnosis output format

After the diagnosis show the author these items, one or two sentences each:
1. The minimal skeleton (answers to the five questions)
2. Main selling points
3. The story engine, and your judgment of how far it can run
4. Suggested length
5. Current gaps and suggestions (at most three, in order of importance)
