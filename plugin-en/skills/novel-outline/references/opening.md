# The opening and the first three chapters

The first three chapters decide whether readers stay. Required reading when planning the cards for chapters 1-3.

## 1. What an opening must deliver, and when

These are common reference points; adjust by genre:

| Progress | What to deliver |
|---|---|
| First 80-150 words | A hook: an anomaly, a crisis, a conflict, or a strong character situation. The reader should know "something is happening" or "this person is interesting" |
| First 700-1,500 words | The selling point shows: the reader can see what pleasure this book mainly offers |
| First three chapters (about 3,000-6,000 words) | The reader knows who the lead is, what trouble they are in, what the book's selling point is, and what they want to see next |

## 2. What chapter 1 (about the first 1,200 words) must establish

1. **The lead**, and the reason the reader should care (wrong done to them, situation, contrast, humor, persistence).
2. **The basic rules of the world**: only what this chapter needs, in a few sentences.
3. **A touch of mystery**: something the reader wants to know a little more about.
4. **A shadow of the core conflict**: even if it is not fully unfolded, the reader should feel the pressure.
5. **Momentum**: the ending must give a reason to open the next chapter.

First make the reader care about the lead, then give the background. Once they care, they will want to know more.

## 3. Common ways the first three chapters progress

Take a growth story with a special advantage:
- **Chapter 1**: create an event. The lead falls into trouble (jilted, framed, thrown out of the house), and the lead-in to the advantage is planted.
- **Chapter 2**: introduce a key person or reveal the advantage, so the reader sees a chance of turning it around.
- **Chapter 3**: the lead uses the advantage for a first gain (a small comeback, a first step of growth), establishing "this is how the book will keep delivering".

Equivalents in other genres:
- **Mystery**: chapter 1 an anomalous event → chapter 2 the investigation hits a wall or a second anomaly appears → chapter 3 the first key clue and higher stakes.
- **Romance**: chapter 1 the two meet with tension → chapter 2 they are forced into contact → chapter 3 a first flutter or the first clear obstacle.

## 4. Common faults of openings

- **Too much background**: long passages introducing the world, family history, the power system. Fix: keep only what the lead's present situation needs, and tell the rest when the plot reaches it.
- **Action, then a stop to explain**: a fight opens, and halfway through come three paragraphs of background. Fix: break the explanation up and tuck it into gaps in the action and dialogue.
- **The whole character dumped at once**: the lead enters with looks, personality and history all explained. Fix: show only a side or two per chapter.
- **Slow burn**: the first three chapters are all daily life with no conflict. Serial fiction defaults to not being slow-burn; if the author is sure, at least hold it up with suspense or character charm.
- **A lead with no agency**: the lead is just pushed by events. Fix: have the lead make at least one choice inside the trouble.
- **Conflict written as a physical problem**: only "how to cross the river", never "why this step matters so much to them". Conflict needs emotional weight.

## 5. Opening planning checklist

After laying out the cards for chapters 1-3, confirm item by item:
- [ ] There is a hook early in chapter 1
- [ ] The reader has a reason to care about the lead in chapter 1
- [ ] The selling point shows within the first three chapters
- [ ] The lead makes at least one active choice in the first three chapters
- [ ] At least one small emotional release within the first three chapters
- [ ] Each chapter ends with a reason to read on
- [ ] World explanation is limited to what is needed now
