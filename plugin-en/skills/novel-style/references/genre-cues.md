# Genre cue table

When the author gives only a genre ("detective novel", "epic fantasy"), pick a starting point here. These are **common starting points, not answers**: every genre has many ways to be written, so when designing ask the author which they want, or offer two directions.

Format: **core tension** (what readers of this genre want) / **common practice** / **where it goes wrong** / **forks to ask the author**.

---

## Mystery / puzzle detective
- Core tension: fairness: the reader gets the same clues as the detective and has the click at the end.
- Common practice: limited viewpoint (detective or assistant); clues hidden in everyday actions and unimportant dialogue without emphasis; description focused on objects and positions; longer sentences and more logical connectives in reasoning passages; tighter pace the chapter before the reveal.
- Goes wrong: the narrator marks the key for the reader ("he didn't notice that this would be crucial"); the detective works by intuition instead of clues; the reveal retells everything in one long speech.
- Forks: classic puzzle or social mystery (motive and background)? Genius detective or ordinary person? May the reader guess before the detective?

## Hard-boiled / noir
- Core tension: a person with principles walking it through to the end in a corrupt world.
- Common practice: first person, cold eye, self-mocking; short sentences, concrete nouns, few adjectives; similes sharp and funny, taken from the street; the city as half a character; dialogue as sparring, with barbs in it.
- Goes wrong: so many similes it becomes a pastiche; a tough guy flat as cardboard; drinking and smoking every chapter.
- Forks: how dense the similes? Where is the lead's moral line? Contemporary or period setting?

## Psychological thriller
- Core tension: uncertainty: the reader doubts what they see alongside the lead.
- Common practice: viewpoint locked to the lead's perception; sentence length follows tension; sense before judgment; self-correction and repetition; dialogue with gaps.
- Goes wrong: the lead calmly analyses their own psychology; an omniscient explanation unmasks it; teaser lines at chapter ends.
- Forks: is the narrator reliable? Does the fear come from an outside threat or from within?

## Horror / the uncanny
- Core tension: a crack in the everyday: something familiar is slightly wrong.
- Common practice: the first half extremely ordinary, the more mundane the detail the better; the anomaly shows only a corner and is not explained; sound and smell are scarier than sight; slow pace, many pauses.
- Goes wrong: describing the monster in full; piling adjectives of dread ("eerie", "sinister", "creepy"); characters screaming.
- Forks: explain the cause or keep the unknown? Folk horror, urban legend or cosmic horror?

## Epic / high fantasy
- Core tension: the rules of an unfamiliar world discovered bit by bit.
- Common practice: the world seeps out of characters' daily life and conflict; restrained proper nouns; magic with limits and costs; description choosing "only this world has it" details.
- Goes wrong: three pages of encyclopedia at the start; every noun capitalized and quoted; people who talk like moderns for no reason.
- Forks: epic or personal scale? Is the world strange to the lead or long familiar?

## Urban fantasy / contemporary paranormal
- Core tension: the hidden world rubbing against ordinary life.
- Common practice: the ordinary described precisely so the intrusion lands; a wry first-person or close-third voice; magic constrained by bureaucracy, cost or secrecy; humor that arises from the clash.
- Goes wrong: constant quips that flatten stakes; lore dumps in dialogue; a "chosen one" with no cost.
- Forks: noir, comic or romantic tone? How much of the world knows?

## Progression fantasy / cultivation / LitRPG
- Core tension: growth and breakthrough: every gain has a cost and a reward.
- Common practice: clear ranks and systems; fights written as cause and effect, not catalogues of move names; training and resource scenes with specifics; payoffs as visible jumps in power.
- Goes wrong: stats dumped as game panels in every chapter (unless deliberate); every opponent sneers the same way; settings read aloud in dialogue.
- Forks: grim or breezy? Is there a literal system interface? How much of the book is training versus conflict?

## Science fiction
- Core tension: an "if" pushed through to the end, and how it changes people.
- Common practice: technology written as use and consequence, not as a manual; new terms arriving naturally in context; calm, precise narration; characters treating technology as casually as we treat phones.
- Goes wrong: information dumps; jargon as show-off; characters existing only to display the setting.
- Forks: hard or soft? Space opera, cyberpunk or near-future realism?

## Romance / contemporary love
- Core tension: two people drawing close and stepping back; readers wait for the moment of confirmation.
- Common practice: close to the heroine (or alternating viewpoints); feeling carried by small actions and objects; dialogue with probing and subtext; changes of physical distance recorded carefully.
- Goes wrong: stock lines like "her heart skipped a beat"; an alpha hero unreasonably domineering; misunderstanding propped up for chapters by silence.
- Forks: sweet, angsty or everyday? Is there a career line beyond romance? How far do intimate scenes go?

## Historical / period fiction
- Core tension: an individual's choices and costs in the current of an era.
- Common practice: period detail in speech, prices and institutions; narration may have a chronicler's calm; big events seen from a small person's angle.
- Goes wrong: modern values applied to characters without noticing; research piled up as footnotes.
- Forks: gaps in recorded history or alternate history? Is the narrator in the time or looking back?

## Literary / contemporary realism
- Core tension: seeing people in the everyday.
- Common practice: detail built from objects; restrained lyricism; emotion leaking out of the cracks of daily life; dialogue trivial but charged between people; open endings.
- Goes wrong: a precious tone; big metaphors; life lessons at chapter ends.
- Forks: cold or warm? Is there a clear plot line?

## Young adult / school
- Core tension: vivid character interplay and a lively pace.
- Common practice: first-person quips, a high share of dialogue, distinct verbal habits; exaggerated reactions; short chapters, lively titles.
- Goes wrong: quipping in every line; characters reduced to a tic; flat pop-culture references that date.
- Forks: how dense the quips? Light or serious underneath?

## Black comedy / satire
- Core tension: an absurd situation met with an over-normal reaction.
- Common practice: deadpan narration; big things played down, small things played up; dialogue logical to the point of absurdity; repetition as comic rhythm.
- Goes wrong: internet one-liners; punchlines spelled out; the narrator laughing first.
- Forks: who is the target of the satire? How cold should it run?

## Regional / rural
- Core tension: land, labour and community.
- Common practice: detail of work and environment; smell, temperature, touch; local speech worked into sentences; emotion pressed down into labour; a slow sense of time.
- Goes wrong: the countryside as a postcard; dialect overused until unreadable; summing-up lyricism.
- Forks: which place and decade? How dense the dialect? Nostalgic or critical?

## Space opera / ensemble epic
- Core tension: the individual in the sweep of history: genius and mediocrity, loyalty and betrayal.
- Common practice: multiple viewpoints, a distant narrator (may have a chronicler's voice); war and politics set side by side; characters defined by what they say and do; aphoristic dialogue that serves a function in the scene.
- Goes wrong: technical lectures on battles; too many characters to remember; a narrator who preaches.
- Forks: is the narrator of the time or looking back? May the lead die an imperfect death?

## Mixed genres

Authors often want a mix ("fantasy + mystery", "romance + suspense"). Method:

1. First ask **which is the skeleton and which the seasoning**: the skeleton decides chapter structure and reader expectation; the seasoning decides local technique.
2. When rules clash, the skeleton wins; say so in the style file: "Reasoning passages may run to longer sentences, but the whole keeps the fantasy's pace."
3. At least one example passage should be a scene where the two meet.
