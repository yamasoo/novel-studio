# The thirteen dimensions of style

Go through these one by one when designing, analysing or revising a style. **Not every item needs filling in**: find the 5-8 that really set this style apart and go deep, and cover the rest in a line or leave them to general rules.

Drawn from several common practices: the style entries of tools like Novelcrafter (style says "how to write", genre says "what to write"; give flexible ranges, not hard numbers), Sudowrite's Style field (labelled examples by scene type), the Voice DNA / style-guide approach (narrative structure, sentence and paragraph structure, diction, rhetoric, dialogue, description, rhythm and transitions, references, an Always / Never / Consider split), and the stylistic checklist of Leech & Short's *Style in Fiction* (lexis, grammar, figures of speech, cohesion and context, speech and thought presentation).

Format of each item: **what to decide** → **what a good rule looks like** → **common traps**.

---

## 1. Positioning and references

- Decide: what the style is in one sentence (the core sentence); who reads it and where (serial platform, print, phone); which works are references and **what to learn from them**; what it must explicitly not resemble.
- A good rule: "Reference: the mystery passages of a classic puzzle writer: learn how clues hide in everyday actions and how a chapter ends on a single question; do not learn the characters' voices."
- Trap: writing only "like such-and-such". The AI imitates the surface (names, tics, signature lines) and may copy text. Always break a reference into describable features.

## 2. Viewpoint and distance

- Decide: first person / third limited / omniscient / rotating viewpoints; tense; how close the narration sits (may it show thoughts, does it dive into the subconscious); when it may pull back or move in; the rules for switching viewpoint (one per scene? a scene-break mark?).
- A good rule: "Third limited, one person per scene; may show their thoughts but never draws their conclusions for them. A change of viewpoint always means a new scene, marked with '◇'."
- Trap: "third person" is not enough; distance makes the biggest difference in how it reads.

## 3. The narrator's stance

- Decide: does the narrator have an attitude (cold eye, warmth, mockery, chronicler, storyteller, unreliable); may they comment, and how often; do they know the ending, may they foreshadow?
- A good rule: "The narrator is the lead looking back, with an occasional 'I didn't know that then', no more than five times in the book; never comments on others' rights and wrongs."
- Trap: left unwritten, the AI defaults to a "gentle voice-over" and pops out with a reflection at the end of the chapter.

## 4. Diction and register

- Decide: formal or plain (literary, conversational, jargon); period feel (the line between period and modern wording); amount of technical vocabulary; regional speech (density of dialect words); loanwords; profanity; forms of address and nouns special to this book.
- A good rule: "Narration in modern plain English, no 'suddenly', no 'couldn't help but'; dialogue may carry regional words with no gloss, the first time cued by action or context so the reader can guess."
- Trap: writing only "concise diction". Say **which kinds of words to use and which not**, ideally with a few examples.

## 5. Sentences

- Decide: rough sentence-length range and variation (ordinary, tense, lyrical); pattern preferences (short, long, parallel, inverted, dropped subjects); punctuation habits (commas stringing long sentences, full stops chopping, how many ellipses and dashes).
- A good rule: "Ordinary narration alternates lengths, from a dozen to forty words; action scenes switch to three-to-eight-word sentences, one action each; no semicolons."
- Trap: hard numbers ("average sentence length 14 words") make the AI pad mechanically. Describe ranges and situations; leave numbers to the "text metrics" section to measure.

## 6. Paragraphs and layout

- Decide: paragraph length, frequency and use of one-sentence paragraphs, whether dialogue gets its own paragraph, scene-break marks, density on a phone screen.
- A good rule: "No paragraph longer than five phone lines; one-sentence paragraphs only for turns or blows, three or four per chapter at most; scene changes get a blank line and '* * *'."
- Trap: the "one sentence per paragraph" habit common in web fiction dilutes emphasis; state **when** a one-sentence paragraph is allowed.

## 7. Description and the senses

- Decide: weight of description; which sense comes first; when description is inserted (one block on entry, or given on the move); how details are chosen (concrete objects, prices, brands, smells); how a character's looks are introduced.
- A good rule: "On entering a new scene, no more than three sentences of description before returning to the character; choose 'something only this place has', never lines like 'sunlight poured in' that fit anywhere."
- Trap: writing only "fine-grained description". Fine-grained is the result; what you must write is **the standard for choosing details**.

## 8. Rhetoric and imagery

- Decide: how many figures of speech and where they come from (grown out of the character's trade, era, world); any recurring image system (rain, doors, clocks); tolerance for hyperbole, irony, parallelism; amount of idiom.
- A good rule: "At most one simile per scene, drawn from what this cook would think of (heat, knife work, sauces), never stars and oceans."
- Trap: left unwritten, the AI reaches for the commonest similes (like a knife, surged like a tide), the biggest source of AI voice.

## 9. Dialogue

- Decide: share of dialogue; tags ("said" mostly, or action beats instead); subtext (the gap between what is said and what is meant); how voices differ; dialect and catchphrases; length of an exchange; whether dialogue may carry setting.
- A good rule: "Tags only 'said' and 'asked', or an action beat; no 'growled' or 'intoned'. Characters do not state their feelings; the reader hears them in the non-answers."
- Trap: dialogue written as information exchange, with everyone talking alike. Write **the method that tells characters apart**.

## 10. Interiority and emotion

- Decide: direct statement or externalizing (action, object, bodily response, choice); the form of inner monologue (direct, free indirect, italics) and its length; the quota of plain statements; how emotional peaks are handled (amplified or played down).
- A good rule: "Do not name the emotion; use one concrete behavior instead. One plain line of feeling is allowed per chapter, colloquial and short, like something blurted."
- Trap: "write no emotion" taken too far goes cold. Give **the alternative** and **the exception quota**.

## 11. Scenes and transitions

- Decide: ratio of scene (moment by moment) to summary (fast-forward); how time jumps are handled; rules for flashback; the pace of disclosure (result first or process first); how a scene opens and closes.
- A good rule: "Two to three scenes a chapter; between scenes a sentence or two of summary carries the time, not 'three days later' opening a paragraph. Enter late and leave early: cut in where the conflict has already begun, and cut away once there is a result."
- Trap: left unwritten, the AI tends to "write everything moment by moment" or "summarize everything".

## 12. Chapter and serial conventions

- Decide: chapter length range; how chapters open (straight in, catching the last hook, a switch of viewpoint); how they end (suspense, emotional afterglow, reversal, plain); whether every chapter needs a small climax or payoff; title style.
- A good rule: "2,000-3,000 words a chapter; the first paragraph must have someone doing something; end on one concrete open question, no reflection, no teaser."
- Trap: mixed in with sentence rules, the AI applies the chapter-ending rule at sentence level (a hook on every paragraph).

## 13. Three-way split of rules: recommended / taboo / allowed exceptions

- **Recommended practice** (Consider): tendencies you may skip when needed. E.g. "an action scene may end on a sound."
- **This book's taboos** (Never): never allowed; doing it is a mistake. E.g. "no reflection at the end of a chapter."
- **Allowed exceptions** (Always-OK): ways of writing the general de-AI rules would block but this book wants on purpose; **always state the purpose**. E.g. "The lead's catchphrase 'Fine' may recur every chapter; it marks the character."
- Trap: all written as taboos and the AI turns stiff in avoiding them; all written as recommendations and it is as if nothing was written.

## Requirements for example passages

Examples are the most powerful part of a style file: what rules cannot make clear, an example shows at once.

- **Labelled by scene type**: at least three, picked from 【對白】【動作】【描寫】【情緒】【轉場】 for what is most characteristic of the style. 60-150 words each.
- **One line "what I like about it" after each**: name the rule it demonstrates, as specifically as possible.
- **One 【反例】**: the same thing written the wrong way, followed by "why it fails". A wrong example draws the boundary precisely, but one is enough; too many and the AI learns the wrong example.
- **Examples must be original or the author's own.** Never copy reference works or use their names or proper nouns.
- When the author gives a sample: prefer the author's passages as examples (excerpts are fine) and fill in the missing scene types.

## Length

The whole style file (not counting the metrics table) should be 1,000-2,000 words. Too short and the AI can only guess; too long and the rules fight and the AI cannot hold them. When over, first cut "what general rules already do" (for example "few typos", "sentences should flow").
