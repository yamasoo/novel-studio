# Right and wrong ways: examples

All examples are original. What you learn is the **structure and method**, not the sentences: do not carry example lines into chapter text.
The examples share one invented situation: Ines Marlow, who keeps the books for her family's ship chandlery in a harbor town, is jilted in public by her fiancé Callum Brandt. She holds half of a brass ferry token her late mother left her.

How to use: read only the group you need right now.

---

## A. Opening hook

### A1 Wrong: starting with background

> The harbor town of Saltreach had stood at the mouth of the Brandt River for three hundred years, and its families were divided into those who owned boats and those who owned the people who sailed them. The Marlows kept a chandlery on Anchor Street. Ines, the younger daughter, had run the ledgers since she was fifteen. Three years ago she had been promised to Callum Brandt, whose father owned half the quay…

**Where it breaks**: three hundred words of background and the reader still does not know what is happening now. Town, family and history are poured out at once and nothing actually happens.

### A2 Right: starting with an event

> The letter breaking off the engagement reached the Marlows' front office while Ines was counting rope.
>
> Piet, the errand boy, ran so hard he left a boot in the doorway. "Miss Ines, the Brandts sent a man. He says — they're saying it's over."
>
> Ines finished the coil, wrote 40 fathoms in the margin, and capped the pen. "Is he waiting?"

**What to learn**: the first sentence is the event (the broken engagement), the second gives contrast (she is counting rope). The boy's panic against her calm makes the reader ask why she is not shaken. No background was given, yet family standing and the engagement are already visible.

## B. Emotion: showing and announcing

### B1 Wrong: announcing the emotion

> At Callum's words, Ines felt an inexpressible mix of sadness and anger. Her dignity had been trampled, and three years of engagement dissolved in an instant. She took a deep breath and tried to stay calm.

**Where it breaks**: sadness, anger, trampled dignity, staying calm are all conclusions handed down by the author. The reader sees nothing concrete.

### B2 Right: action and object

> Ines said nothing.
>
> She looked at the letter on the desk. When the engagement was settled, her mother had still been alive and had edged the paper with a thin line of gold ink, because it looked cheerful. The gold had gone the colour of mustard.
>
> She turned the letter over so the gold edge faced the wood.

**What to learn**: the emotion sits on an object (the gold line her mother drew). The turning over carries all at once: she cares, she does not want anyone to see she cares, she is cutting the past loose. Not a single emotion word.

### B3 Right: one plain line is allowed

> She had thought she wouldn't cry.
>
> It happened while she was still adding the invoice for the tar, the pen halfway down a column, and a drop landed on "three shillings sevenpence" and spread.

**What to learn**: one plain statement of feeling (she had thought she wouldn't cry), caught at once by a concrete action. Banning all direct statement turns precious; at the right place, one line is enough.

## C. Dialogue

### C1 Wrong: instruction-manual dialogue, one voice

> "Ines, I have come today to end our engagement," Callum said. "Because you cannot join the shipping guild and I am about to take my father's seat on the board, the gap between us has grown too wide."
>
> "I understand what you mean," Ines said calmly. "Although I am very sad, I respect your decision. I hope everything goes well for you in the future."

**Where it breaks**: the two state their motives and feelings in full, every line a complete, polite, logical, written-style sentence. With nothing hidden there is no tension; cover the names and you cannot tell who is speaking.

### C2 Right: subtext and different voices

> Callum slid the letter an inch toward her. "The guild dinner is the third of next month."
>
> "Congratulations."
>
> "Your father keeps the deposit, of course. We wouldn't want it said that —"
>
> "The seal on this," Ines said. "Yours, or your mother's?"
>
> Callum's fingers stopped on the desk.

**What to learn**: Callum never says "break off"; he talks about the dinner and the deposit because he wants to end it politely. Ines answers in one word, then cuts in with a question that is itself an attack (implying he cannot decide for himself). The reply is a stopped hand; there is no need to write that he feels guilty. Their speech differs completely: one circles, one goes straight.

### C3 Right: not answering directly

> "That token of your mother's," Aunt Dorrit said, smoothing Ines's collar with a smile. "I hear only half of it is left?"
>
> "It's turning cold," Ines said. "Is your cough any better, Aunt?"

**What to learn**: the question is dodged and answered with another. The reader sees both sides probing and nobody saying it aloud.

## D. Transitions

### D1 Wrong: the reader gets lost

> Ines turned and left the front office. The apothecary was crowded, and the clerk said that herb was sold out.

**Where it breaks**: from the office to the apothecary, nothing says how long it took, how she got there, or what mood she carried. The reader stops: when did she go out? Why the apothecary?

### D2 Right: time, place and mood caught in one sentence

> Half an hour later Ines stood at the counter of Hollis & Daughter, the letter still folded in her sleeve, one corner pressing into her wrist.
>
> "That sea-fennel from last month," she said. "Is there any left?"

**What to learn**: "half an hour later" gives time, "the counter of Hollis & Daughter" gives place, and "the letter … pressing into her wrist" carries the last scene's mood over. Three jobs in one sentence.

---

## E. Character-state continuity

### E1 Wrong: the injury vanishes

> (Previous chapter: to save Ines, Callum's left hand was cut open by a hook.)
>
> Next morning Callum drilled with the oars in the yard, rowing smooth and strong, stroke after stroke.

**Where it breaks**: hurt yesterday, no effect today. Readers remember.

### E2 Right: the body's state shapes the action

> Next morning Callum was at the oars in the yard. Right hand only.
>
> At the stroke where he should have switched grips, he stalled, the blade made a stiff half-circle in the air, and he began again from the top.

**What to learn**: the wound need not be explained again, but it affects the movement. "Right hand only", "stalled" and "began again" let the reader recall the injury and see his stubbornness.

---

## F. The payoff scene: the chain of reactions

### F1 Wrong: cheering on the reader's behalf

> Ines took out the half token. Everyone was stunned and could not believe their eyes. Aunt Dorrit's face changed, and Callum stood rooted. In that moment, everyone who had looked down on her regretted it.

**Where it breaks**: "everyone was stunned" and "regretted it" are the author drawing the conclusion for the reader. The crowd gets one line and nobody has a specific reaction, so the thrill cannot appear.

### F2 Right: different people, different reactions

> Ines set the half token on the letter.
>
> The first sound came from old Mr. Pell in the corner. He got halfway out of his chair, sat back down, and said something nobody caught.
>
> Aunt Dorrit was still smiling; the smile stayed on her face, forgotten.
>
> Callum reached for the brass. Before his fingers touched it, Ines drew the letter out from under, and the token slid into her palm.
>
> "I'll keep the letter," she said. "This, you don't get to look at."

**What to learn**: three people, three reactions: Mr. Pell recognizes it (half rises, sits back), Aunt Dorrit cannot react in time (the forgotten smile), Callum wants to confirm (the reach). The thrill comes from others' perceptions flipping, closed with one line from the lead that also leaves a question (why can't he look?).

## G. Chapter-ending hooks

### G1 Wrong: an uplifting ending

> Ines closed her hand around the token and looked out at the darkening harbor. She knew that from this moment her life would never be the same. Her reckoning was only beginning.

**Where it breaks**: "from this moment" and "only beginning" are empty foreshadowing; the reader gets no concrete expectation.

### G2 Right: an open question

> The brass felt warmer against her palm than it had a minute ago.
>
> Ines turned it over. On the broken edge, where the metal had always been smooth, a line of letters had appeared, so fine it might have been scratched in that minute:
>
> THE BRANDTS OWE MORE THAN A LETTER.

**What to learn**: stopping on a concrete discovery that throws up a question the reader must have answered.

### G3 Right: a crisis

> She had just blown out the lamp when footsteps sounded in the yard.
>
> Not Piet. Piet dragged his heels.
>
> The footsteps stopped outside her door.

### G4 Right: a light teaser (suits a bridge chapter)

> Ines tucked the token into the inside pocket of her coat and told Piet: "First thing tomorrow, harness the cart. We're going to Hollis & Daughter."

**What to learn**: not every chapter needs danger. A bridge chapter gives a clear next step; the reader knows what happens tomorrow and has a reason to open the next chapter.

### Rotating hooks

If several chapters in a row end in G3-style crises, readers go numb. Rotate crisis, question, reversal, new goal, light teaser, and alternate strong with soft.
