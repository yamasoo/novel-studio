# Craft for writing chapter text

Read once before writing chapter text (no need to reread within the same conversation). This is general craft; where it conflicts with this book's `設定/文風.md`, the style file wins.

## 1. Scenes: every scene must change something

A scene = **goal → obstacle → change**.
- Open so the reader knows who wants what in this scene.
- Make the obstacle concrete: a person, a sentence, a door, a rule.
- By the end the situation has changed: the relationship, the information, the circumstances, or the goal.

After a scene, ask: if I cut it, what would the story lose? If the answer is "nothing", rework it.

## 2. Emotion: show it, do not announce it

This is where AI voice shows most. Let the reader feel the emotion instead of being told.

**Three ways to land it:**
1. **Action**: what the character does under the emotion. Not "she was nervous" but she turned the teacup three times and never drank.
2. **Object**: attach the emotion to something concrete. Not "he missed his mother" but he still kept the apron washed pale, hanging behind the door where no one dared touch it.
3. **Reaction chain**: stimulus → first bodily reaction → thought or choice → action. Do not skip the middle and jump to the conclusion.

**Judgment:**
- An occasional plain statement of feeling, placed where it is needed most, can hit harder. Banning all direct statement turns precious.
- Big emotion does not mean heavy description. The more it hurts, the shorter the sentences and the fewer the adjectives, usually.
- Do not attach a micro-expression to every emotion (a twitch of the mouth, a flicker in the eyes, dilating pupils). Micro-expressions are seasoning, not the main dish.

## 3. Dialogue

### Every line does at least one thing
Advance the plot, reveal character, create tension, or pass on information (and bury that inside conflict). Small talk and pure information handover: cut or rewrite.

### Subtext
Real people rarely say what they mean outright. When a character is hiding something, the dialogue has tension.

Five channels that let the reader see the gap between surface and truth:
1. **Words**: the position they state (which may be false).
2. **Body**: involuntary small movements.
3. **Physiology**: pulse, damp palms, a tight throat (only the viewpoint character can feel these).
4. **Inner voice**: the viewpoint character's real reaction.
5. **Voice**: pace, volume or pauses that suddenly change.

When the reader sees the gap between these channels, no narrator needs to explain. Subtext must be readable to the reader even if the other characters in the scene miss it.

### Everyone must sound different
- Write to the character's voice card (the "way of speaking" in `設定/角色/`).
- Test: cover the speaker's name. Can the reader guess who is talking?
- Not everyone is articulate: some fumble, some answer a different question, some say half a sentence.
- Not every question gets answered: dodging, changing the subject and asking back all play better than a straight answer.

### Dialogue tags
- "Said" is the best dialogue verb. Do not swap it for "growled", "intoned", "murmured coldly".
- When it is obvious who is speaking, drop even "said"; an action beat can carry the attribution.
- Do not hang an adverb like "with a hint of …" on every line.

## 4. Transitions

Readers get lost most at scene changes. Each transition should make clear:
- **Time**: how long has passed? ("half an hour later", "next morning")
- **Space**: where are we now? Orient with one concrete sensory detail, not a paragraph of setting.
- **Emotional carry-over**: how does the last scene's feeling continue or turn in the new one? Characters do not forget what just happened the moment the scene changes.
- **Viewpoint**: if the viewpoint character changes, the first sentence should tell the reader whose eyes these are.

Quick cuts are fine, but never cut the causality, the character state or the relationship.

## 5. Writing the satisfying payoff scene

1. **The set-up is identifiable**: before the burst, the reader can see the lead's preparation or the pressure being held down.
2. **A clean strike**: write the key action short, exact and visual; do not pile adjectives into "presence".
3. **The chain of reactions is the body of the thrill**: write the different reactions of those present: the stunned, the unconvinced, the regretful, the reassessing. The thrill comes from other people's perceptions flipping.
4. **Give a result**: after the fight, what did the lead actually get (reputation, resources, standing, information)?
5. **Do not cheer on the reader's behalf**: no summary lines like "In that moment everyone was stunned". Let the reactions speak.

## 6. Rhythm and sentences

- **Alternate long and short sentences.** All long is stifling; all short reads like a telegram. Short when tense, longer when easing.
- **Paragraphs need to breathe.** On a phone, long paragraphs drive readers away. Ordinary narration changes paragraph every 3-5 lines; an important line may stand alone, but not every line.
- **Description follows action.** Setting works best when a character notices it mid-action and it bears on their present situation. Description that could move into any scene is filler.
- **Metaphors: exact and few.** The best come from the character's own life (a butcher who sizes people up like cuts of meat, a bookkeeper who sees people as ledgers). Stock metaphors (like a tide, like a blade, the gears of fate): cut if you can.

## 7. Boundaries of disclosure

- Respect "what the reader knows now" in `追蹤/時間線.md`. The narrator cannot give away a truth that has not been revealed, nor sneak in a preview with "little did he know".
- In limited viewpoint, write only what the viewpoint character can see, hear and think.
- Respect the card's prohibitions.
- Do not use an ability or trump card before it unlocks.

## 8. Three kinds of AI voice to check after writing

The check tool cannot catch these; reread paragraph by paragraph:

1. **Naming the point for the reader**: a tidy parallel line, an aphorism or a triplet that sums up what the paragraph means.
   → Cut it. If the reader cannot follow without it, the showing was weak; go back and add action or detail.
2. **The narrator stepping out to explain**: summarizing the character's feeling ("she finally understood …"), explaining motive ("the reason he did it was …"), writing the card's conclusion straight into the text.
   → Turn it into the character's action, dialogue or objects and let the reader see it.
3. **An uplifting, reflective or foreshadowing chapter ending**: "It would be a long night for everyone", "She did not know that a greater storm was coming", "From this moment, everything was different."
   → Stop on a concrete image, an action, or one line of dialogue.

Also check:
- **Leaked workshop words**: the text must not contain "chapter X", "the previous chapter", "foreshadowing", "chapter card", "the reader" and the like (unless the story really has such things).
- **Copying the card**: the card's plot points are instructions to you, not ready-made sentences. Dramatize them as scenes instead of stringing the card's sentences together in other words.
- **English stock phrases**: a shiver ran down her spine; a breath she didn't know she was holding; something shifted in the air; the weight of it settled; for the first time in years; not X, but Y as a reflex; a smile that didn't reach his eyes. Replace each with something specific to this scene.

## 9. Chapter endings

- Stop on a **change**, not a summary.
- Stop on a concrete image, action or line.
- Use the hook type the card planned; if the card is silent, choose by the chapter's function (see the chapter-ending hooks in novel-outline: open question, crisis, reversal, new goal, light teaser, emotional afterglow).
- The next chapter's first beat must be able to catch this ending.
